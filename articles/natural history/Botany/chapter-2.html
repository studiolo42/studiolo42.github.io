§ 1

The Chemistry of Life

Life is chemistry. Every process in a living cell — growth, movement, reproduction, perception — is at its core a chemical reaction. To understand plants, we must first understand the molecules from which they are built and the rules that govern how those molecules behave.

Plant cells are composed primarily of four types of large molecules called macromolecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. These are built from smaller subunits (monomers) linked together into large polymers. This principle — small, repeating units assembled into large functional structures — is one of the great unifying themes of biochemistry.

All four classes share a fundamental pattern: they are built by condensation reactions (also called dehydration synthesis) — reactions that release a water molecule (H₂O) each time two monomers are joined. They are disassembled by the reverse process, hydrolysis, which adds water to break bonds. This elegant reversibility allows cells to build and break down molecules as needed.

§ 2

Carbon — The Backbone of Organic Chemistry

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon-containing compounds — and carbon is the element that makes life possible. Of the 118 known elements, only carbon has the combination of properties that allows the construction of molecules of sufficient size, complexity, and stability to serve as the machinery of life.

Schematic 2 · Atomic Properties
Why Carbon? — Four Unique Properties

Carbon bonds with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur — the five other elements that together with carbon make up more than 98% of living matter. The variety of carbon compounds is effectively limitless: chemists have identified over 10 million distinct organic compounds, and life uses perhaps a few hundred thousand of them.

Key Functional Groups on Carbon Skeletons
—OH   Hydroxyl  ·  —COOH   Carboxyl  ·  —NH2   Amino  ·  —CHO   Aldehyde  ·  —C=O   Ketone  ·  —PO43−   Phosphate  ·  —SH   Sulfhydryl
Each functional group confers specific chemical properties: —COOH is acidic; —NH₂ is basic; —OH is polar and hydrophilic; —CH₃ (methyl) is hydrophobic.

Isomers deserve special attention: two molecules with identical molecular formulas but different structural arrangements have completely different properties. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) and fructose (C₆H₁₂O₆) are structural isomers — same atoms, different arrangement, different biological roles. This specificity of molecular shape is fundamental to how biological molecules work.

§ 3

Water — The Medium of Life

If carbon is the skeleton of life, water is its blood, sweat, and medium. Approximately 80–90% of a living plant cell's mass is water. Every biochemical reaction in the cell takes place in aqueous solution. The properties of water are so extraordinary — and so perfectly suited to life — that many scientists consider the existence of life without it essentially impossible.

The Water Molecule
H2O    Molecular weight: 18 g/mol    Bond angle: 104.5°    Density: 1.0 g/cm³ (liquid at 4°C)
Two O—H covalent bonds. Oxygen is more electronegative → partial negative charge (δ−) on O, partial positive (δ+) on each H. This polarity is the source of water's remarkable properties.
Schematic 3 · Water Structure & Properties
The Water Molecule — Structure, Polarity & Hydrogen Bonds
Water Ionisation & pH
H2O  ⇌  H+  +  OH−
Kw = [H+][OH−] = 1 × 10−14 mol²/L²
pH = −log[H+]   Neutral: pH 7  ·  Acidic: pH < 7  ·  Basic: pH > 7
Most plant cellular processes operate optimally between pH 6.5–7.5. The cytoplasm is buffered to maintain this range. Vacuoles can be highly acidic (pH 3–5) for storage and defence.
Cohesion–Tension Theory (Relevance to Plants)

Water's cohesion (H₂O molecules sticking to each other) and adhesion (sticking to xylem walls) create a continuous column of water from root to leaf. Transpiration from leaves creates a tension that pulls this column upward. In a tall tree, water rises 100m against gravity — powered not by pumps, but by the hydrogen bonds in water. This elegant mechanism (the SPAC: Soil–Plant–Atmosphere Continuum) is unique to plants.

§ 4

Carbohydrates — Energy & Structure

Carbohydrates are the most abundant organic molecules on Earth — primarily because of cellulose, the structural material of plant cell walls. Their name comes from the general formula Cn(H₂O)n: hydrates of carbon. They serve plants in two fundamental roles: energy storage (starch, sucrose) and structural support (cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin).

General Carbohydrate Formula
Cn(H2O)n  or equivalently   (CH2O)n
n = 3 (triose) · n = 5 (pentose: ribose, ribulose) · n = 6 (hexose: glucose, fructose, galactose)

Carbohydrate Formula Type Role in Plants
Glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ Monosaccharide (hexose) Primary product of photosynthesis; fuel for cellular respiration
Fructose C₆H₁₂O₆ Monosaccharide (hexose, isomer of glucose) Fruit sweetness; component of sucrose and inulin
Ribose C₅H₁₀O₅ Monosaccharide (pentose) Backbone of RNA; component of ATP, NAD⁺, FAD
Sucrose C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ Disaccharide (glucose + fructose) Primary transport sugar in phloem; table sugar
Maltose C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ Disaccharide (glucose + glucose, α-1,4) Starch breakdown intermediate; germinating seeds
Starch (amylose) (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ Polysaccharide (α-glucose, unbranched) Energy storage in plastids, seeds, tubers
Starch (amylopectin) (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ Polysaccharide (α-glucose, branched at α-1,6) Energy storage; branching allows rapid mobilisation
Cellulose (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ Polysaccharide (β-glucose, unbranched) Primary cell wall; most abundant organic polymer on Earth

Starch vs Cellulose — Same Formula, Opposite Functions
Condensation Reaction: Forming a Glycosidic Bond
Glucose (C6H12O6) + Glucose (C6H12O6) → Maltose (C12H22O11) + H2O
For sucrose (transport sugar): Glucose + Fructose → C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O. Sucrose is non-reducing (both anomeric carbons involved in bond), making it chemically stable for long-distance transport in phloem.
§ 5

Lipids — Membranes, Waxes & Energy Storage

Lipids are a chemically diverse group united by one property: they are hydrophobic — they do not dissolve in water. This repulsion from water is not a defect but a feature exploited by evolution to create membranes, waterproof coatings, and energy-dense storage molecules.

Fatty Acid — Saturated
Palmitic Acid

All C—C single bonds. Straight, linear chain. Packs tightly → solid at room temp. Found in seeds and storage fats.

One C=C double bond → cis kink in chain. Cannot pack tightly → liquid at room temp. Membrane fluidity.

Three C=C bonds. Very fluid. Essential fatty acid. Abundant in chloroplast membranes — required for photosynthesis.

§ 6

Proteins — The Workhorses of the Cell

Proteins are the most functionally diverse molecules in biology. They serve as enzymes (catalysts), structural materials, transporters, signals, receptors, motors, and regulators. Every phenotypic difference between species ultimately traces to differences in proteins. They are built from just 20 amino acid monomers, linked into chains that fold into specific three-dimensional shapes.

The General Amino Acid Structure
H2N—CH(R)—COOH
H₂N— = amino group (basic)  ·  central α-carbon  ·  —COOH = carboxyl group (acidic)  ·  R = variable side chain (determines identity & properties)
Peptide bond: —CO—NH— (formed by condensation between COOH of one + NH₂ of next, releasing H₂O)
Schematic 7 · Structural Hierarchy
The Four Levels of Protein Structure

The most important protein in plant biology is Rubisco (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) — the enzyme that fixes CO₂ in the Calvin cycle. It is the most abundant protein on Earth, constituting roughly 25% of the nitrogen in leaves. Its molecular formula (for the complete enzyme) is approximately C₂₄₇₀H₃₈₈₈N₆₆₄O₇₈₀S₂₁ — a protein of 540 kDa assembled from 16 polypeptide subunits.

Peptide Bond Formation
R1—NH2 + HOOC—R2 → R1—NH—CO—R2 + H2O
The peptide bond (—CO—NH—) is a partial double bond — it has ~40% double-bond character, making it rigid and planar. This rigidity constrains protein backbone geometry and forces secondary structure into specific patterns (α-helices, β-sheets).
Enzyme

A protein catalyst that lowers activation energy. Named with suffix -ase. Highly specific — one enzyme, one reaction (or small family of reactions).

Active Site

The specific region of an enzyme where the substrate binds. Precise shape and chemistry. Lock-and-key or induced-fit models.

Denaturation

Loss of 3D structure due to heat, pH extremes, or chemicals. The primary sequence remains, but function is lost. Usually irreversible.

Coenzyme

Non-protein molecule required for enzyme activity. Often derived from vitamins. Examples: NAD⁺, FAD, Coenzyme A.

§ 7

Nucleic Acids — The Language of Heredity

Nucleic acids carry the genetic instructions for building and operating every living cell. There are two types: DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) stores the genetic programme, and RNA (ribonucleic acid) transcribes and translates it into proteins. The molecular logic underlying all inheritance — in every organism that has ever lived — is written in the four-letter language of nucleotide bases.

The Nucleotide — Monomer of Nucleic Acids
Nucleotide = Phosphate Group (—PO₄3−) + Pentose Sugar (ribose or deoxyribose) + Nitrogenous Base
DNA sugar: deoxyribose (C₅H₁₀O₄) — missing one —OH at 2' position
RNA sugar: ribose (C₅H₁₀O₅) — has —OH at 2' position
The polymer backbone: 5'→3' phosphodiester bonds connecting sugars

The Central Dogma of Molecular Biology
ATP — Adenosine Triphosphate (Energy Currency)
ATP  =  Adenosine + 3 phosphate groups  ·  Molecular formula: C10H16N5O13P3
ATP + H2O → ADP + Pᵢ + 30.5 kJ/mol
ATP is a nucleoside triphosphate — essentially the adenine nucleotide of RNA, but with two extra phosphate groups. The hydrolysis of the terminal phosphate anhydride bond releases ~30.5 kJ/mol of free energy, which is used to drive endergonic reactions throughout the cell.
§ 8

Secondary Metabolites — The Plant's Chemical Arsenal

Plants cannot run from predators. Instead, over hundreds of millions of years, they have evolved an extraordinary repertoire of chemical defences — secondary metabolites. These compounds are not directly involved in primary metabolism (growth, reproduction) but serve as deterrents, attractants, UV screens, and antimicrobials.

Plants produce over 200,000 distinct secondary metabolites — a number that exceeds the total output of the entire pharmaceutical industry. Many of our most important medicines are plant secondary metabolites or derived from them.

Schematic 10 · Classification & Key Formulas

Why Secondary Metabolites Matter to Humans

More than 60% of anticancer drugs approved since 1940 are either natural products from plants or derived from them. Aspirin (salicylic acid, C₉H₈O₄) was derived from willow bark. Quinine (from cinchona bark, C₂₀H₂₄N₂O₂) fought malaria for centuries before synthetic drugs. Taxol from the Pacific yew treats ovarian and breast cancer. Every time we discover a new plant secondary metabolite, we discover a new molecule shaped by hundreds of millions of years of evolutionary pressure — a head start that no pharmaceutical designer can replicate.
