§ 1

What Is Biology?

Biology — from the Greek bios (life) and logos (study) — is the scientific study of life in all its forms. It asks the most fundamental questions that can be asked about the natural world: What is alive? How does it work? How did it come to be? How does one living thing relate to all others?

Botany is the branch of biology dedicated to plants — and it is one of the oldest sciences. The ancient Greeks, particularly Theophrastus (371–287 BCE), a student of Aristotle, produced the first systematic descriptions of plants. His works Historia Plantarum (Inquiry into Plants) and De Causis Plantarum (On the Causes of Plants) remained the standard botanical references for nearly 1,500 years.

"The desire of knowledge, like the thirst of riches, increases ever with the acquisition of it." — Laurence Sterne (1713–1768)

Today botany encompasses plant morphology (form and structure), plant physiology (how plants function), plant ecology (how plants interact with their environment), plant taxonomy (how plants are classified), and plant evolution (how plants have changed through time). These are not separate subjects — they are interlocking lenses on the same living systems.

§ 2

Characteristics of Living Organisms

Before we can study life, we must ask: what is life? This is deceptively difficult. A flame consumes fuel and produces heat and matter — but it is not alive. A crystal grows and has an orderly structure — but it is not alive. What distinguishes the living from the non-living?

Biologists have identified a set of properties that, taken together, characterise all known living organisms. No single property is sufficient — viruses replicate but cannot metabolise independently; fire consumes energy but does not evolve. It is the full constellation of properties that defines life.

🔋
Ordered Complexity

Living things are highly organised — atoms → molecules → organelles → cells → tissues → organs → organisms. This order is maintained against the thermodynamic tendency toward disorder.

Entropy must be actively resisted
⚡
Metabolism

All living things capture and transform energy. Plants do this primarily through photosynthesis — converting light energy into chemical energy stored in sugars. This energy drives all cellular processes.

Anabolism + Catabolism = Metabolism
📈
Growth & Development

Living organisms grow and develop according to a genetic programme. A plant grows from a single cell (the zygote) into a complex multicellular organism via cell division, elongation, and differentiation.

Directed by the genome
🔄
Reproduction

All living things reproduce, passing genetic information to offspring. Plants do this both sexually (flowers, seeds) and asexually (runners, rhizomes, vegetative propagation). Reproduction is not individual survival — it is species continuity.

Sexual · Asexual · Vegetative
🌡️
Response to Stimuli

Living things respond to environmental signals. Plants are exquisitely sensitive — they grow toward light (phototropism), toward gravity (gravitropism), and respond to touch (thigmotropism). The Venus flytrap snaps shut in milliseconds.

Tropisms · Turgor · Signalling
⚖️
Homeostasis

Life maintains internal stability despite external fluctuation. Plants regulate water balance (osmosis, stomatal opening/closing), internal pH, and temperature within functional limits. Regulation requires constant energy expenditure.

Dynamic equilibrium maintained
🔀
Evolution & Adaptation

Populations of living things change over generations through natural selection. This is the key insight that unifies all of biology. Adaptations — traits that increase fitness — accumulate over time.

Darwin · Wallace · 1859
🧬
Heredity

Living things inherit traits from their parents via DNA. The genetic code — A, T, G, C — is universal across all of life. This universality is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that all life shares a common ancestor.

DNA → RNA → Protein

What Is a Plant?

Intuitively, we all know what a plant is — the oak tree, the grass, the rose, the seaweed. But a precise scientific definition is surprisingly subtle. The kingdom Plantae as currently understood includes approximately 390,000 known species, and botanists continue to discover thousands more each year.

Key Insight

Plants are autotrophs — they make their own food from inorganic compounds using energy from sunlight. This single fact is what makes plant life foundational: without autotrophs, no heterotrophs (animals, fungi, most bacteria) could exist. Every food chain on Earth ultimately traces back to photosynthesis — to a plant, alga, or cyanobacterium capturing a photon of light.

§ 4

The Kingdoms of Life

How do we organise the 8–10 million estimated species on Earth into a coherent system? This is the problem of taxonomy — the science of classification. The modern system traces back to Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778), who introduced the binomial nomenclature still used today: every species has a two-part Latin name, e.g. Homo sapiens or Quercus robur.

The modern classification recognises a hierarchy of nested groups. The broadest level is the Domain (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya), then Kingdom, and successively more specific levels: Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.

Schematic 4 · Hierarchical Diagram
Taxonomic Classification — from Domain to Species
Taxonomy

The science of naming, describing, and classifying organisms based on shared characteristics and evolutionary history.

Binomial Nomenclature

The two-part Latin naming system: Genus + species (always italicised). Introduced by Linnaeus in 1753.

Phylogeny

The evolutionary history and relationships among organisms, now reconstructed using molecular data (DNA sequences).

Clade

A group consisting of an ancestor and all of its descendants — the basic unit of modern (cladistic) classification.

§ 5

Evolution & Natural Selection

Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) is the most important book in the history of biology. Its central insight — that species change over time through the mechanism of natural selection — unified all of biology and explained the diversity of life without invoking supernatural causes.

Darwin observed four undeniable facts about the natural world and drew from them a logical conclusion of staggering power:

Darwin's Four Observations → One Conclusion

It is crucial to understand what natural selection does not imply. Evolution has no direction or goal. Organisms do not evolve toward perfection. Natural selection simply means that, given heritable variation and differential survival, the composition of a population will change over time. That is all — and it is everything.

"There is grandeur in this view of life... from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved." — Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species, 1859
§ 6

The Cell Theory

The cell theory is one of the foundational principles of all of biology. It was developed in the mid-19th century through the work of Matthias Schleiden (plants, 1838), Theodor Schwann (animals, 1839), and Rudolf Virchow (cell division, 1855). Together, their work established three fundamental principles that remain unchanged today.

Schematic 6 · Three-Pillar Diagram
The Three Tenets of Cell Theory

The third tenet — omnis cellula e cellula ("every cell from a cell") — was Virchow's revolutionary insight. It ruled out the concept of spontaneous generation — the idea that living things could arise from non-living matter — which had been accepted since Aristotle. Today, we understand that the first cells arose approximately 3.8 billion years ago through abiogenesis, but that every subsequent cell has arisen only from a pre-existing cell.

§ 7

The Scientific Method

Science is not a collection of facts — it is a method for generating reliable knowledge about the natural world. The scientific method is the systematic process by which scientists ask questions, test hypotheses, and build knowledge.

Crucially, science progresses by falsification, not by proof. A hypothesis is scientific only if it can, in principle, be shown to be wrong. We can never prove a theory — we can only fail to disprove it. The more a theory withstands attempts at falsification, the more confidence we have in it. Evolution and cell theory have withstood 160+ years of rigorous testing.

Schematic 7 · Cyclical Process
The Scientific Method — A Continuous Cycle
Hypothesis vs Theory — A Critical Distinction

In everyday language, "theory" means a guess. In science, a theory is an explanation that has been tested and supported by substantial evidence — it is the highest level of scientific confidence. Evolution is a theory in this sense: not a guess, but a well-established explanation supported by evidence from genetics, palaeontology, comparative anatomy, biogeography, and ecology. A hypothesis, by contrast, is a testable prediction made before gathering evidence.

§ 8

Plant Diversity & Evolution Timeline

The plant kingdom as we know it today is the result of approximately 500 million years of evolution — from the first tentative colonisation of land by algae-like ancestors to the extraordinary diversity of flowering plants (angiosperms) that dominate most of Earth's terrestrial environments today.

Each major transition in plant evolution solved a problem posed by the terrestrial environment: desiccation, reproduction without water, structural support against gravity, and dispersal across dry land. These transitions are not just historical curiosities — they are the foundation for understanding everything else in botany.

~3,500 Million Years Ago
Cyanobacteria — The First Photosynthesisers

Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) become the first organisms to perform oxygenic photosynthesis. Over the following billion years, they pump oxygen into Earth's atmosphere, transforming the planet and making complex multicellular life possible. The Great Oxidation Event (~2,400 Mya) is their legacy.

~1,200 Million Years Ago
Green Algae (Chlorophytes) Arise

The green algae emerge — aquatic photosynthetic eukaryotes. Crucially, the charophytes (a group of green algae) are the closest living relatives of land plants, and share several key biochemical and developmental features with them. All land plants descended from charophyte ancestors.

~470–500 Million Years Ago · Ordovician
First Land Plants — The Conquest of Land

Small, non-vascular plants resembling modern liverworts colonise damp areas near water. This is one of the most dramatic transitions in evolutionary history. These plants lack true roots, stems, and leaves — but they have cuticles (waxy coatings to prevent desiccation) and sporopollenin (UV-resistant spore walls). The land is transformed.

~430 Million Years Ago · Silurian
Vascular Plants — Internal Plumbing

Plants evolve vascular tissue: xylem (for water transport upward) and phloem (for sugar transport). This allows plants to grow tall — no longer limited by diffusion distance. The first vascular plants (Cooksonia) are simple branching stems, but they represent an enormous leap in plant architecture.

~360 Million Years Ago · Devonian
Seeds — Reproduction Freed from Water

The evolution of the seed is arguably the most important innovation in plant history. Seeds enclose the embryo in a protective coat with its own food supply, freeing plants from the need for water for fertilisation. The first seed plants (pteridosperms, or seed ferns) dominate the late Devonian landscape.

~250–65 Million Years Ago · Mesozoic
Gymnosperms — The Age of Conifers

The gymnosperms (conifers, cycads, ginkgos, gnetophytes) dominate Mesozoic landscapes alongside the dinosaurs. "Gymnosperm" means "naked seed" — the seeds are exposed on cone scales, not enclosed in a fruit. Today's giant redwoods and Scots pines are gymnosperms, unchanged in fundamental anatomy for hundreds of millions of years.

~130 Million Years Ago · Early Cretaceous
Angiosperms — The Flowering Revolution

The flowering plants (angiosperms) appear and diversify explosively — Darwin called their rapid rise "an abominable mystery." The angiosperm revolution is tied to the co-evolution of flowers and pollinators (insects, birds, bats). Seeds enclosed in fruits allow more effective dispersal. Today, angiosperms comprise ~90% of all plant species — some 300,000 species.

Schematic 8 · Evolutionary Sequence
The Major Transitions in Plant Evolution

Understanding this evolutionary sequence is not merely historical context — it is the key to understanding why plants are structured the way they are. Every feature of a plant's anatomy or physiology, from stomata to wood to flowers, is an evolutionary solution to a problem that plants encountered during their long history on land.

"Nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution." — Theodosius Dobzhansky, 1973
